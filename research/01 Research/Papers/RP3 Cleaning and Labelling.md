# RP3 – Data Cleaning, Alignment and Labelling

## Abstract

RP3 answers eight questions on turning raw prices, text, macro and graph data into leak-free training sets, from 92 sources (3 Strong, 46 Moderate, 26 Weak, 17 primary documentation). **Leakage (RQ3.1)**, ranked by judgement across separate studies whose sizes are in different units: features or executions that use information after the decision time (Sharpe +15 to +26 for linear, tree and graph models in one benchmark), index-membership and survivorship errors (several points a year), missing delisting returns, then restatements; memorisation in a price-only token model is untested. **Text (RQ3.2)**: vendor timestamps ran late (by about 2.4 hours for over half of matched analyst records, NYSE 2002–2007) and after-close text belongs to the next session; the effect of deduplication and entity linking on forecasts is unmeasured. **Prices (RQ3.3)**: standard filters and query-time corporate-action adjustment are well documented, but how much each step changes a forecast is not. **Imputation (RQ3.4)**: impute from the past only and flag it; reconstructing 25 years of 1-minute bars from daily data is not supported. **Labels (RQ3.5)**: no label method is shown to be better; crash thresholds must use past data only. **Normalisation (RQ3.6)**: use same-date ranks or backward windows; in one CSI 300 study, removing price-level offsets carried most of the gain; on the data tested, fitting scalers on all data mattered far less than future-reaching windows, test-set selection or overlapping labels. **Graphs (RQ3.7)**: stamp every edge with valid dates and the date it became knowable. **Identity (RQ3.8)**: key on dated permanent IDs, never tickers or names, and keep knowledge time ourselves, because CRSP rewrites history in place and FactSet's supply-chain data records no knowledge time. Confidence is Medium for most answers and Low wherever effect sizes come from one study.

## Research questions

- **RQ3.1** – What are the main sources of look-ahead leakage (restatements, timestamp misalignment, survivorship, index membership, model memory), and how large are they?
- **RQ3.2** – How accurate are text timestamps? How should after-hours news be mapped, syndicated stories deduplicated, and mentions linked to companies?
- **RQ3.3** – What are standard cleaning procedures for prices: corporate actions, bad ticks, delisted stocks, trading at different times?
- **RQ3.4** – When does imputing missing financial data help, and when does it harm? What are the risks of training or backtesting on synthetic or imputed history (including the 25-year 1-minute idea in the archived autoencoder chat)?
- **RQ3.5** – How should labels be defined: fixed horizon, triple barrier or trend scanning? How do studies define a crash or shock, and how do they handle rare classes?
- **RQ3.6** – How should non-stationary features be normalised, and how do preprocessing steps leak information across folds?
- **RQ3.7** – How are time-valid company graphs built from filings, news and price co-movement?
- **RQ3.8** – How should entity identity be kept over time (name and ticker changes, mergers, spin-offs, reorganisations)? How should records keep valid time (when a fact was true) apart from knowledge time (when it became known)?

RP3 must include: a ranked list of leakage sources with reported sizes, a recommended-practice table per data type (prices, text, macro, graph), the label definitions found with their trade-offs, and a direct answer on the imputation question.

## Findings

### RQ3.1 – What are the main sources of look-ahead leakage (restatements, timestamp misalignment, survivorship, index membership, model memory), and how large are they?

**Answer.** Every source the guide names has a measured effect, but the sizes differ by an order of magnitude, and one (model memory in numeric models) can go either way. Ranked by the size reported:

1. **Decision-time misalignment inside the pipeline** is the largest and the easiest to commit. With the split already chronological, centered rolling features or entering at the open while using that day's full bar raise backtest Sharpe by roughly 15–26 points for Ridge, LightGBM and graph models and about 4–9 for LSTM and Transformer models (clean Sharpe 0.3–0.9), positive in every test year. In the same benchmark, normalising on the full sample or building the graph with future data added almost nothing (Sharpe gain at most 0.3) ([[Zhang 2026 When Alpha Disappears Decision-Time Leakage]]).
2. **Index membership and survivorship** inflate returns by several points a year. Using end-of-period top-500 US stocks instead of start-of-period ones adds 1.5–7.2% a year by decade on CRSP 1926–2006 (6.4% vs 2.3% a year in 2001–2006) ([[Daniel 2009 Look-Ahead Benchmark Bias]]). In Indian small caps, today's members overstate 2016–2025 returns by 4.9 points a year; 82.5% of stocks ever in the index had left it, most by graduation or demotion rather than delisting ([[Ranse 2026 Survivorship Bias NIFTY Smallcap 250]]).
3. **Missing delisting returns** matter for anything holding small or distressed stocks. Over 90% of delisted stocks have no delisting-month return in CRSP's monthly file; merging the separate delisting returns cuts the lowest asset-growth decile by about 34 bp a month (about 4% a year), while value-weighted results barely move ([[Fu 2014 Asset Growth Anomaly Delisting Bias]]). About 13% of delistings still lacked a delisting return in 2017 ([[Liu 2020 Data Quality Problems Finance Databases]]).
4. **Restated or re-standardised fundamentals.** In Compustat Snapshot data the sign of earnings changes flips in about 9–10% of firm-quarters across updates, and earnings-surprise hedge returns differ by about 25–31 points depending on when the data were collected ([[Lyle 2024 Re-Standardized Financial Statement Data]]).
5. **Macro revisions.** Revisions are large (2008Q4 US real GDP growth −3.8% first, −6.2% later) and real-time evaluation weakens or reverses several published forecasting results ([[Croushore 2011 Frontiers of Real-Time Data Analysis]]).
6. **Timestamp misalignment between label time and availability time.** Picking the latest record by its label (issue) time chose a record not yet published at decision time on over 99% of days for six of ten weather-model feeds at one gate; the apparent accuracy gain ranged from −9% to +24%, so the sign is not known in advance ([[Alfalah 2026 Point-in-Time Leakage Electricity Data]]; electricity data, but the failure transfers to news, filings and vendor archives; equities evidence is under RQ3.2).
7. **Model memory.** For LLMs reading text it is real and large: GPT-4o recalls pre-cutoff index levels and prices almost exactly ([[Lopez-Lira 2025 LLM Memorization Problem]]), a vintage that has seen the outcome period earns a significant look-ahead premium ([[Yan 2026 DatedGPT Time-Aware Pretraining]]), and a blinded, identity-masked test cut a validated signal's IC from 0.065 to 0.020 ([[Dehkordi 2026 OLIVIA LLM News Look-Ahead Test]]). One study found the opposite inside its sample, with anonymised headlines doing better ([[Glasserman 2023 Look-Ahead Bias GPT Sentiment]]). For **numeric time-series foundation models**, which is closer to the project's autoregressive model, training on post-origin data changed forecasts materially but usually made them worse (higher error in 18 of 20 US cases; certainty-equivalent return −1.8 to −2.1 points a year) ([[Chen 2026 Look-Ahead Bias Pretrained Forecasting Models]]). Exposure is therefore an information-set violation to remove, not a reliable inflation to subtract.

Two RP1 notes add context. A review defines point-in-time as a stronger condition than "out of sample": features, universe membership, preprocessing and model parameters must all exclude later-only information ([[Zhu 2026 AI Equity Crypto Limits Review]], Weak, used as a map, not as evidence). A negative example combines two leaks at once: news summaries written after the fact and prices z-scored on full-period statistics gave a commodity-spike AUC of 0.94, falling to 0.46 without the news ([[Ghali 2025 Commodity Price Shocks Fusion]], Weak).

**Confidence:** Medium. Each leakage type is documented, but most sizes come from one or two studies in one setting. The ranking (pipeline misalignment > membership/survivorship > delisting > restatements) is a judgement across separate studies with different units; no study compares them in one panel, and the magnitudes are not portable.

### RQ3.2 – How accurate are text timestamps? How should after-hours news be mapped, syndicated stories deduplicated, and mentions linked to companies?

**Answer.** (1) **Timestamps are often wrong in a known direction: vendor times run late.** When daytime analyst recommendations and guidance in I/B/E/S and First Call were checked against newswires, about 56–58% of those that could be matched had an earlier true time, by about 2.4 hours on average; with vendor times an upgrade's 30-minute reaction is −0.07%, with newswire times +1.83% ([[Bradley 2014 Analyst Recommendation Timestamp Delays]]). For filings, acceptance time is not public time: EDGAR releases filings begun after 5:30 p.m. (10 p.m. for Forms 3/4/5) the next business day ([[SEC 2026 Accessing EDGAR Data]]), and in 2012–13 paying feed subscribers saw about half of Form 4 filings seconds before the website, with prices moving about 30 s before public posting ([[Rogers 2017 Run EDGAR Run SEC Dissemination]]). A record's label time (issue time) can precede its availability by hours ([[Alfalah 2026 Point-in-Time Leakage Electricity Data]]). Which outlet reports first depends on the events sampled, so there is no fixed "social leads news" lag ([[Bazyari 2026 Breaking-News Latency Sample Effects]]). (2) **After-hours text belongs to the next session.** More than 40% of earnings announcements come after hours and their reaction appears one trading day after the database date ([[Berkman 2009 After-Hours Earnings Event Day 0]]); prices now jump after 91% of announcements in after-hours trading ([[Christensen 2026 Warp Speed Jumps After Earnings]]). Aggregating posts by calendar day instead of by the 4 p.m. close creates spurious next-day predictability ([[Gabrovsek 2017 Twitter Sentiment Earnings Announcements]]). Much of the news move happens before and on the publication day ([[Kargarzadeh 2026 When Is News Priced In]]), so a wrong day boundary converts reaction into apparent prediction. (3) **Repeated stories carry less information and can mislead.** News with high textual overlap with earlier stories moves prices much less, and its move partly reverses ([[Tetlock 2011 Stale News Reprint]]); straight reprints are discounted, but recombinations of old facts provoke over-reactions that reverse ([[Fedyk 2023 When Market Identifies Old News]]). Near-duplicates are also a named leakage channel: random splits inflate news-direction MCC 1.1–6.5× over chronological splits, and masking firm names improves several event types, exposing name memorisation ([[Xue 2026 Temporal Leakage Financial News NLP]]). No study measured how much a specific deduplication rule improves a forecast. (4) **Entity linking.** Coreference and subsidiary rules raised company-mention recall from 57% to 77% at a precision cost (100% → 90%), but did not make news predict prices better than the majority class ([[Passonneau 2014 Company Mention Detection]]). Character n-grams with suffix normalisation plus short context beat string matching for enterprise linking, though on a test set with the hardest name pairs removed ([[Ding 2021 JEL Entity Linking JPMorgan]]). No study tested linking accuracy over time (renamed or merged firms); that is RQ3.8.

**Confidence:** Medium for timestamp lateness and after-hours mapping (several independent studies agree on the direction). Low for deduplication and entity linking: their effect on forecasts is unmeasured, and the linking evidence is weak.

**What would change the answer:** a measured comparison of news-vendor timestamps (e.g. RavenPack, Dow Jones, Benzinga) against exchange-time price reactions after 2015; a test of a deduplication or novelty rule's effect on an out-of-sample forecast; a point-in-time entity-linking benchmark covering renames and mergers.

Top-up list: Berkman & Truong (2009) full text; Levi, Livnat, Zhang & Zhang (2016) on extended-hours news (seen only in a summary); a study of syndication clustering in news vendors (none found).

### RQ3.3 – What are standard cleaning procedures for prices: corporate actions, bad ticks, delisted stocks, trading at different times?

**Answer.** The procedures are well established for high-frequency data and for delistings; the evidence on *how much* each step matters is thin and mostly comes from single-stock studies. (1) **Bad ticks and intraday filters.** The standard TAQ recipe keeps regular-hours records from one exchange with non-zero prices, merges same-timestamp records by median, drops negative or extreme spreads (over 50× the daily median), corrected and abnormal-condition trades, trades outside the quote by more than a spread, and quotes far from a rolling median ([[Barndorff-Nielsen 2009 Realised Kernels in Practice]]). Very few records are bad, but they matter: an outlier filter that removed 0.18% of one stock-month's trades changed every coefficient of a duration model and the mean duration from about 7 to 9.4 minutes, and dirty-vs-clean differences exceeded differences between filter settings ([[Brownlees 2006 Ultra-High Frequency Data Handling]]). Both recipes use centred windows, which is fine for cleaning history but must be trailing in any live feature (the same violation that inflated backtests most in [[Zhang 2026 When Alpha Disappears Decision-Time Leakage]]). (2) **Corporate actions.** Keep raw prices and apply adjustment factors at query time, so both versions exist; vendors differ (query-time raw/split/dividend/spin-off adjustment at Alpaca; pre-built adjusted and unadjusted series at Norgate) ([[Alpaca 2026 Market Data API Stocks]], [[Norgate 2026 US Stocks Packages and Delisted Data]]). Vendor adjusted columns can be wrong: Alpha Vantage's adjusted close was inconsistent against CRSP while raw closes matched ([[Giulio 2025 Alpha Vantage Adjusted Close vs CRSP]]), and even CRSP's factors are wrong around property dividends, spin-offs and rights offerings ([[Liu 2020 Data Quality Problems Finance Databases]]). (3) **Delisted stocks.** Use a survivorship-free source with permanent identifiers and merge delisting returns ([[CRSP 2026 US Stock Databases]]); omitting them biases distressed portfolios by about 4% a year ([[Fu 2014 Asset Growth Anomaly Delisting Bias]]), and exits through demotion and graduation bias results as well as true delistings ([[Ranse 2026 Survivorship Bias NIFTY Smallcap 250]]). Delisting *dates* are themselves error-prone: warning, decision, last-trading and effective dates are easily confused, and even web-enabled audits dated at most 46% of delistings exactly ([[Yao 2026 DelistBench Corporate-Event Database Completion]]). (4) **Listing windows and gaps.** Never back-fill before listing: padding with the first price cut measured volatility by about 20% and GARCH variance by about 26% across 53 instruments ([[Muhammad 2026 Temporal Coverage Bias Panel Data]]). (5) **Different trading times.** Daily closes from markets with different hours understate cross-market correlation (DJIA–Nikkei 0.19 → 0.33 after synchronising to the New York close) ([[Audrino 2002 Synchronizing Multivariate Financial Time Series]]); the alternatives are synchronising to one clock, using weekly data, or modelling the lag. Entering at a time before the information in a bar existed (same-day open with the day's high, low and close) is the extreme case ([[Zhang 2026 When Alpha Disappears Decision-Time Leakage]]).

**Confidence:** Medium for the procedures (consistent across sources and implemented in standard tools). Low for the size of each step's effect: the intraday evidence is one stock, and the corporate-action evidence is one informal check plus a review.

### RQ3.4 – When does imputing missing financial data help, and when does it harm? What are the risks of training or backtesting on synthetic or imputed history (including the 25-year 1-minute idea in the archived autoencoder chat)?

**Answer.** Imputation helps when it lets a model keep observations that would otherwise be dropped, uses only information available at the time, and is flagged; it harms when it uses future values, invents values the data cannot pin down, or replaces real history. (1) **Firm characteristics and fundamentals.** Missingness is systematic, not random: more than 70% of firms (about half of market cap) lack at least one of 45 characteristics, small firms and extreme values are missing more often, and returns differ by whether a value is observed (up to 8.1% a year for sales-to-price) ([[Bryzgalova 2025 Missing Financial Data]]). Dropping incomplete rows keeps only about 10% of firm-months in an 82-characteristic panel and biases results; cross-sectional mean or median filling is inconsistent and shrinks standard errors ([[Freyberger 2025 Missing Data Asset Pricing Panels]]). A cross-sectional factor model plus the firm's own previous value roughly halves the error of median filling (block-masked RMSE 0.14 vs 0.29) without look-ahead ([[Bryzgalova 2025 Missing Financial Data]]). Most of the measured gain, though, comes from keeping the full universe, not from the imputation method: in Freyberger et al. the Sharpe ratio rises from 1.19 (complete case) to 1.66 with crude mean filling and only to 1.79 with conditional-mean filling. In general tabular data, better imputation buys about a tenth as much better prediction, and missingness indicators help even when values are missing at random ([[Le Morvan 2024 Imputation for Prediction Diminishing Returns]]). (2) **Look-ahead through imputation.** Imputing with the full panel, a future value (backward-forward models, next-observation-carried-backward, bidirectional networks) or a centred smoother leaks the test period into training ([[Blanchet 2023 Imputation Look-Ahead-Bias Variance Tradeoff]], [[Bryzgalova 2025 Missing Financial Data]]). ACT-Tensor's doubled factor Sharpe ratio comes from a centred moving average on a whole-sample tensor fit, with delisted stocks dropped, so it is not evidence of a usable gain ([[Mo 2025 ACT-Tensor Financial Panel Imputation]]). Common toolkits score imputers by masked-value error and do not test look-ahead ([[PyPOTS 2026 Partially Observed Time Series Toolbox]]). (3) **Prices.** Filling before a listing date or across gaps creates zero returns that cut measured volatility by about 20% ([[Muhammad 2026 Temporal Coverage Bias Panel Data]]); carrying stale quarterly values forward is itself imputation and can create mechanical predictability ([[Bryzgalova 2025 Missing Financial Data]]). (4) **Synthetic history.** Generated data helps only where the target has persistent structure and then only slightly: volatility forecasts improve by ΔR² ≈ +0.011 for linear models and ≈ 0 for random forests, while every generator hurts daily direction prediction, TimeGAN and VAE most ([[Dezons 2026 Synthetic Augmentation Financial ML]]). TimeGAN scenarios understate volatility and tail losses and get cross-asset correlations wrong ([[He 2026 TimeGAN Scenarios Rachev Portfolios]]); realism scores do not predict usefulness downstream ([[Hashimoto 2026 Synthetic Scenario Compatibility Hedging]]); generators lose the tails first ([[Shumailov 2024 Model Collapse Training on Generated Data]]); and a model that never saw an extreme event in training does not extrapolate to it ([[Sun 2024 AI Weather Gray Swan Cyclones]]).

**The 25-year 1-minute idea (archived chat, [[Autoencoders I]]).** Not supported by the evidence. The plan observes 1-minute bars for the last 5 trading days (about 0.08% of 25 years, roughly 6,300 trading days), hourly for 2 years and daily beyond, and asks a TST-LSTM autoencoder to fill every missing minute back to the start. Four problems follow from the sources above. (a) The information is not there: a daily OHLC bar pins down about as much of the day's variance path as 2–3-hour sampling, and measurement error falls more than 200-fold from daily to 5-minute sampling ([[Andersen 1998 Answering the Skeptics Volatility]]), so the minute path inside a historical day is not identified by the data; any filled path is a draw from the model, not recovered history. (b) A model trained on such bars learns the generator, which under-represents tails and cross-asset structure ([[He 2026 TimeGAN Scenarios Rachev Portfolios]], [[Shumailov 2024 Model Collapse Training on Generated Data]]), and a backtest on them measures the generator, not the market. (c) The pipeline as written fills NaNs with 0 before training and uses bidirectional context, which creates zero returns and look-ahead ([[Muhammad 2026 Temporal Coverage Bias Panel Data]], [[Blanchet 2023 Imputation Look-Ahead-Bias Variance Tradeoff]]). (d) Five days of 1-minute data from one recent regime cannot represent minute-level behaviour in 2001 or 2008. The "99.999% accuracy" premise conflates reconstruction error on masked bars with forecasting value, which are weakly linked ([[Le Morvan 2024 Imputation for Prediction Diminishing Returns]], [[Hashimoto 2026 Synthetic Scenario Compatibility Hedging]]). No study was found that trains a minute-level model on minute bars reconstructed from daily bars and validates it on real minute data (evidence gap). The supported alternatives are real intraday history from a vendor (RP2 shortlist), models at the frequency the data has (daily features with range-based volatility, intraday only where it exists), and explicit masks for unobserved periods.

**Confidence:** Medium for characteristic and fundamental imputation (two large RFS studies plus a general benchmark; all agree on direction). Medium that synthetic data rarely helps return forecasting (three finance studies, small samples). Medium that the 25-year 1-minute reconstruction should not be used as training or backtest data (mechanism well established; no direct test).

### RQ3.5 – How should labels be defined: fixed horizon, triple barrier or trend scanning? How do studies define a crash or shock, and how do they handle rare classes?

**Answer.** (1) **Label method.** The evidence does not show that any one labelling method is better; it is thin and mostly tuned on the data it is tested on. The only side-by-side test of all three found by this search (16 assets, 2000–2025) gave average Sharpe ratios near or below zero for fixed horizon (−0.29), triple barrier (−0.03) and trend scanning (0.00), with costs and parameters undisclosed ([[Kili 2025 Adaptive Event-Driven Labeling]]). Triple-barrier labels beat direction labels at 15- and 30-minute bars but not at 5-minute bars, and results swing with barrier width and holding time, which were explored on the test period ([[Bieganowski 2024 Supervised Autoencoder Triple Barrier]]). In practice barrier parameters are often chosen to balance classes (29 days, ±9% on Korean stocks), not for forecasting value ([[Kang 2025 Triple Barrier OHLCV Korean Stocks]]). For fixed-horizon labels, the horizon that matches the target is not always the best training label: for next-day returns a much shorter intraday label trained better, for 30-minute targets the target itself was best ([[Song 2026 Label Horizon Paradox]]). So the label horizon is a hyperparameter to choose on validation data, and path-dependent or overlapping labels need purging in validation (RP4, RQ4.1). Fee-aware thresholds (no-trade unless the move clears costs) make most labels "no trade" (about 80% at 30 seconds) ([[Zhao 2025 Label Unbalance High-Frequency Trading]]). (2) **Crash and shock definitions.** Three families were found: a return below a low quantile of the return distribution (1st percentile of an expanding window, [[Andrew 2024 CRISIS ALERT Market Crisis ML]]; 5/2.5/1% VaR quantiles of the full sample, [[Siswara 2024 Early Crash Detection ASEAN-5]]); a volatility spike above a training-period percentile (95th, [[Dezons 2026 Synthetic Augmentation Financial ML]]); and a dated crisis chronology (banking crises from bank-equity crashes plus narrative, [[Greenwood 2022 Predictable Financial Crises]]). Thresholds computed on the full sample use future data: in Greenwood et al., using only backward-looking thresholds lowered the high-risk effect from 38.2 to 34.1 percentage points. No study comparing definitions was found. (3) **Rare classes.** Without any treatment, sequence models never flagged a crash (hit rate 0); with SMOTE-ENN they caught about 21% of crash days with PR-AUC about 0.07 at a 1% base rate ([[Siswara 2024 Early Crash Detection ASEAN-5]]). Class-weighted losses beat under-sampling and focal loss in one HFT study ([[Zhao 2025 Label Unbalance High-Frequency Trading]]); cost-weighted decision thresholds (false negatives 20× false alarms) are the simpler route ([[Andrew 2024 CRISIS ALERT Market Crisis ML]]). Targeted oversampling can raise average precision for spikes but conflicts with unconditional significance tests ([[Dezons 2026 Synthetic Augmentation Financial ML]]). F1, MCC, accuracy and balanced accuracy push alarm thresholds to the edges as events get rarer; proper scoring rules (Brier, log score) for model choice plus a fixed false-alarm/miss cost for the threshold are recommended instead ([[Nikolopoulos 2025 Rare-Event-Stable Evaluation Metrics]]).

**Confidence:** Low for choosing between fixed horizon, triple barrier and trend scanning (few, weak, partly conflicting sources). Medium that crash thresholds must be computed from past data only. Medium for rare-class practice (class weights or cost-weighted thresholds, proper scoring rules), Low for the size of the gain.

### RQ3.6 – How should non-stationary features be normalised, and how do preprocessing steps leak information across folds?

**Answer.** (1) **Normalise with information available at the decision time, per date or per window.** For cross-sectional stock features the standard practice is to rank each characteristic across stocks every period and map ranks to [−1, 1], filling gaps with that period's cross-sectional median ([[Gu 2020 Empirical Asset Pricing Machine Learning]], rev1 p. 24); this uses no other dates, so it cannot leak across time. For sequence inputs, normalising each window by its own backward-looking statistics helps most: on a point-in-time CSI 300 five-minute panel it raised next-day rank IC for all eight parameter-matched encoders (+0.014 to +0.057, Holm-significant), more than the gap between encoders; the gain came from removing each stock's price-level offset (centering, last-value or differencing), while scale-only normalisation did nothing ([[Chen 2026 Centering Drives Normalization Gains]]). A learned per-window shift and scale did the same on limit-order-book data (Cohen's κ 0.31 global z-score → 0.49) ([[Passalis 2020 Deep Adaptive Input Normalization]]). Without any normalisation, models failed outright in every study that tested it (κ ≈ 0 on FI-2010; zero-shot MASE 9.38 vs 1.02 with RevIN, [[Ahmed 2025 Normalization Zero-Shot TSFMs]]). Models with no built-in normalisation gain most from first differencing (MLP MASE 31.9 → 3.1 on M4, [[Padilha 2026 Preprocessing Impact Forecasting Benchmarks]]); fractional differencing beat log returns in one index study, but only on price-level errors with no naive baseline ([[Stempien 2025 Fractional Differencing LSTM Indices]]). (2) **Autoregressive (next-token or next-patch) models need causal statistics.** Normalising every patch with whole-window statistics leaks the future during parallel training; running (causal) statistics plus an arcsinh transform gave the best forecasts at contexts of 256–1024, statistics from the first k patches were best at short contexts ([[Vilhes 2026 Normalization Causal Large Time-Series Models]]). For token models trained with cross-entropy (Chronos), the choice among mean/scale normalisations barely mattered once inputs were scaled ([[Ahmed 2025 Normalization Zero-Shot TSFMs]]). No study tested these choices on a market-data token model. (3) **How preprocessing leaks across folds, largest first.** (a) Features computed from windows that reach past the decision time (centered rolling windows) were the largest leak in a one-switch finance benchmark (Sharpe +15 to +26), while fitting normalisation on the full sample added only 0.00–0.04 ([[Zhang 2026 When Alpha Disappears Decision-Time Leakage]]). (b) Choosing models or seeds on test results (+0.040 and +0.045 AUC) and duplicates (up to +0.07) far outweigh preprocessing leakage; fitting scalers, PCA or encoders on all data changed AUC by under 0.005 on 2,047 iid datasets ([[Roth 2026 Which Leakage Types Matter]]). (c) On 49,799 news articles, random instead of chronological splits inflated next-day direction MCC by 1.1× to 6.5×, more for bigger models ([[Xue 2026 Temporal Leakage Financial News NLP]]); shuffled k-fold with overlapping labels invents skill: R² 0.83–0.91 on a target built to be unpredictable, below the mean baseline once purged ([[purgedcv 2026 Purged Cross-Validation Library]]); random folds on data with real drift added +0.023 AUC ([[Roth 2026 Which Leakage Types Matter]]). (d) Imputing train and test together plants a signal that downstream models exploit ([[Blanchet 2023 Imputation Look-Ahead-Bias Variance Tradeoff]]); it overturned every civil-war paper claiming complex ML beat logistic regression ([[Kapoor 2023 Leakage Reproducibility Crisis ML Science]]). (e) Feature selection on all data matters when features are many relative to rows (+0.018 AUC at p/n > 0.1, [[Roth 2026 Which Leakage Types Matter]]). So per-fold fitting of scalers is correct but cheap insurance; the costly leaks are future-reaching feature windows, test-set selection, overlapping labels and global imputation. A caveat: global statistics on a trending, non-stationary series carry future level information, so the "negligible" result from iid data should not be assumed for raw prices; fit on the training period and refit per walk-forward step.

**Confidence:** Medium that normalisation should use only same-date cross-sections or backward windows, and that offset removal is the active part for price inputs (one well-designed finance study scored Moderate 9, one LOB study, several general studies). Medium for the ranking of leak sizes (two direct measurements, one in finance). Low for which normalisation suits an autoregressive market-token model (general data only).

### RQ3.7 – How are time-valid company graphs built from filings, news and price co-movement?

**Answer.** Three families of construction were found, each with its own time stamp and its own failure. (1) **Filings and curated databases.** The classic route uses customers that firms must disclose above 10% of sales (Compustat segment files): 30,622 firm-year links over 1980–2004, 78% across industries ([[Cohen 2008 Economic Links Predictable Returns]]), but only about a thousand links a year and skewed by the 10% rule ([[Bacilieri 2023 Firm-level Production Networks]], [[Compustat 2026 Historical Segments Customers]]). Commercial graphs (FactSet Revere, from 2003) record start and end dates per relationship, i.e. valid time, but not when the vendor learned of the link, so back-filled links can leak ([[FactSet 2026 Revere Supply Chain Relationships]]); they also miss most links (mean degree under 10 vs about 40 in complete administrative data, [[Bacilieri 2023 Firm-level Production Networks]]), and a GNN trained on Bloomberg's graph found public evidence for 21% of the links it predicted were missing ([[Gopal 2021 Supply Chain Link Discovery GNN]]). LLM extraction from 10-Ks can stamp each edge with its filing date and reached 88% audited precision for stronger edges, but 28% of disclosed exposures name only a category, and any edge drawn from the model's own knowledge is not point-in-time ([[Yang 2026 LLM Point-in-Time Economic Graphs]]); LLM-built graphs used in return tests carry the same risk ([[Zhou 2026 LENS LLM Priors News Graphs]]), and web-snippet extraction found 9.3 times more links than a disclosure database with precision unmeasured ([[Fukada 2026 Snippet-Driven Supply Chain Discovery]]). (2) **News and analysts.** Same-sentence co-mentions in Reuters (2006–2013) gave 20,504 links among 2,961 firms, each dated by publication, covering all overlapping Compustat customer links and most text-similarity and co-search links, and its connectivity forecast volatility and spreads one month ahead ([[Schwenkler 2020 Network of Firms Implied by News]]). A graph of firms covered by the same analyst in the past 12 months (IBES) is easy to date and its neighbour-momentum feature subsumed customer, industry and other link momentum ([[Ali 2020 Shared Analyst Coverage Momentum Spillover]]). (3) **Price and trading co-movement.** Correlation networks must use trailing windows stamped at the window's end: four-year rolling correlation trees are stable between monthly steps but reorganise around crashes, with edge half-life ≈ 0.12 × window length ([[Onnela 2003 Dynamic Asset Trees Market Correlations]]); a rolling correlation graph helped GNN volatility forecasts slightly while sector and Granger graphs did worse than HAR ([[Wade 2026 GNN Volatility Forecasts Portfolios]]); a graph learned on an expanding window from momentum features worked only gross of costs ([[Pu 2023 Network Momentum Across Asset Classes]]); daily co-trading networks track covariance beyond sectors and drift from GICS over 2017–2019 ([[Lu 2024 Co-Trading Networks US Equities]]). (4) **Size of the leak.** Building the graph with future data inflated Sharpe by up to 0.32 in a one-switch benchmark, far less than future-reaching feature windows (+15 to +26) but not zero ([[Zhang 2026 When Alpha Disappears Decision-Time Leakage]]). Practice that follows: store every edge with source, valid-from/valid-to and the date it became knowable (filing acceptance time, article time, vendor first-seen date); rebuild the graph as of each training date from edges knowable then; stamp co-movement graphs at the last day of their trailing window; keep survivorship-free node sets; treat LLM-inferred edges as not point-in-time unless the model's cutoff precedes the date.

**Confidence:** Medium for the construction rules (consistent across sources; mostly descriptive). Low for the size of graph look-ahead (one benchmark) and for how much a more complete graph improves forecasts.

### RQ3.8 – How should entity identity be kept over time (name and ticker changes, mergers, spin-offs, reorganisations)? How should records keep valid time (when a fact was true) apart from knowledge time (when it became known)?

**Answer.** (1) **Identity.** Key every record on a permanent identifier and treat tickers, names and CUSIPs as dated attributes. Tickers are reused (the ticker "S" passed from Sears to Sprint Nextel in 2005 and to SentinelOne in 2021) and names change, so joins on ticker or name alone are wrong; CRSP's names file gives each spell a start and end date ([[Stanford GSB 2026 CRSP Names File FAQ]]). CRSP separates the security (PERMNO) from the company (PERMCO), and the company ID usually survives mergers but changes in some reorganisations, so cross-database links must carry date ranges and are partly matched by hand ([[NY Fed 2018 CRSP-FRB Link Documentation]], [[CRSP 2026 US Stock Databases]]). Free identifiers cover only part of the problem: OpenFIGI includes delisted equities only on request ([[OpenFIGI 2026 API Documentation]]); GLEIF parent links start in May 2017 ([[GLEIF 2026 Level 2 Who Owns Whom]]); EDGAR gives a CIK with current and former names, but its documentation does not show dates on former names ([[SEC 2026 EDGAR Submissions API]]). Corporate-event dates in vendor data are themselves unreliable: public-source audits got delisting status right 97% of the time but exact announcement dates for at most 46% of true delistings ([[Yao 2026 DelistBench Corporate-Event Database Completion]]). Name-to-entity linking is accurate on easy news mentions (F1 0.999, hardest cases excluded, [[Ding 2021 JEL Entity Linking JPMorgan]]) but company clustering across languages reached only about 33% recall even with a better retriever, and that study dropped entities with successors ([[Qi 2026 Multilingual Company Entity Resolution]]); no study found tests linking across renames, mergers or spin-offs. (2) **Valid time vs knowledge time.** The standard model keeps valid time (when a fact was true) and transaction or record time (when it was stored or learned) as separate axes ([[Jensen 1995 TSQL2 Data Model]]); record history is append-only while actual history may be corrected ([[Fowler 2021 Bitemporal History]]). Vendors do not do this for the user: CRSP rewrites values "for entire history", moves data between PERMNOs and re-codes reorganisations with new IDs, listing changes but not keeping old versions ([[CRSP 2017 Annual Release Notes Data Edits]]). So the project has to supply knowledge time itself: store each vendor extract as a dated vintage (as RP2 found) or in a bitemporal store with `AS OF` queries ([[XTDB 2026 Time in XTDB Bitemporal]]); for each fact keep valid-from, valid-to, known-from (publication or first-seen time), superseded-at, source and vintage; build training and backtest sets "as known at t" and use "as true at t" only for analysis.

**Confidence:** High that tickers and names are not identities and that joins need dated permanent IDs (all documentation agrees). Medium for the bitemporal design (standard definitions, consistent vendor evidence, no finance study testing it). Low for how large identity errors are in forecasting results (thin: no study measured it).

## Cross-cutting observations

- **One rule underlies most answers:** every value used at time t must have been knowable at t. Leakage (RQ3.1), timestamps (RQ3.2), normalisation (RQ3.6), graphs (RQ3.7) and identity (RQ3.8) are the same failure in different data types, and the largest measured effects come from breaking it inside the pipeline, not from data vendors.
- **Vendors supply valid time, rarely knowledge time.** Delisting dates, supply-chain links, CRSP histories and news timestamps are all revised or back-filled without a record of when the revision happened (RQ3.2, RQ3.7, RQ3.8; RP2). The project has to stamp first-seen times and keep vintages itself.
- **Effect sizes are thin and local.** Most magnitudes come from one study, one market or one period; the rankings are more portable than the numbers.
- **Cheap safeguards beat clever fixes.** Keeping the full universe matters more than the imputation method (RQ3.4); removing price offsets matters more than the encoder (RQ3.6); past-only thresholds matter more than the label scheme (RQ3.5).
- **Evidence by family:** cleaning and leakage studies are mostly Moderate (designed measurements); graph-construction and identity evidence is mostly documentation; label and synthetic-data studies are mostly Weak.

### Recommended practice by data type

## Contradictions and evidence gaps

- **Model memory direction (RQ3.1):** text models gain from seeing the future (Yan 2026, Lopez-Lira 2025), numeric foundation models usually lose (Chen 2026); no test of a price-token autoregressive model.
- **Gaps:** one-panel comparison of all leakage types (RQ3.1); measured effect of deduplication and entity linking on forecasts (RQ3.2); multi-stock evidence on how much each price-cleaning step changes a forecast (RQ3.3). No study tests a model trained on intraday bars reconstructed from daily bars against real intraday data (RQ3.4); no walk-forward, cost-aware comparison of fixed-horizon, triple-barrier and trend-scanning labels on a broad cross-section, and no comparison of crash definitions (RQ3.5). Top-up lists are in each RQ's stop-rule line.
- **RQ3.6 gap:** no finance study compares normalisation schemes for the same model under walk-forward validation on a broad panel, and none tests normalisation for a market-data token model; the "global scaling is harmless" result comes from iid data and one finance benchmark.
- **RQ3.7 gap:** no study measures the lag between a relationship starting and it appearing in filings, news or vendor databases, or compares forecasts from a strictly as-known graph with a back-filled one.
- **RQ3.8 gap:** no study measures the forecasting error caused by identity mistakes (ticker joins, current-name joins) or by replacing as-known vintages with a current vendor extract; entity-resolution studies exclude renamed and merged entities.

## Assumptions made during the research

- Exa search tools are not connected in this session; alphaXiv was used first, then built-in web search and page fetch (guide fallback).
- For studies that measure a bias rather than test a forecaster, rubric checks that do not apply (out-of-sample, baselines, costs) are marked N/A and the total scaled to 14, as in RP2.
- RQ3.1: LLM look-ahead notes from RP1 (Lopez-Lira 2025, Yan 2026, Dehkordi 2026, Glasserman 2023) are reused for their lessons only; no new LLM-specific search was run, following the 2026-11-07 scope update.
- RQ3.1: Zhang 2026's Sharpe gains of 15–26 are mechanical artefacts of impossible trades; they are used to rank leakage types, not as realistic sizes.
- RQ3.1: Fu (2014) was read through a summarising page fetch, so no page numbers are given; Shumway (1997) is cited only through Fu and Liu because its PDF failed to load.
- RQ3.2: Bradley (2014), Passonneau (2014) and Fu (2014) were read through a summarising page fetch; Berkman & Truong (2009) from its abstract only (scored Weak for that reason).
- RQ3.2: "After hours" is taken as the US convention (after the 4 p.m. ET close and before the 9:30 a.m. open); exchanges with other sessions need their own boundaries.
- RQ3.3: Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2009), Brownlees & Gallo (2006) and Audrino & Bühlmann (2002) were read through a summarising page fetch from authors' or working-paper versions; journal versions were not opened.
- RQ3.3: the claim that vendor-reported delisting replacement returns of about −30% to −55% are standard is left out of the answer because the original papers could not be opened.
- RQ3.1: Croushore (2011) is a survey scored on only three applicable checks (5/6 scaled to 12/14). Its Strong label reflects the evidence it collects, not one test; read the macro-revision row with that in mind.
- RQ3.1: Ranse (2026) reports p < 0.001 alongside a bootstrap result that implies about p = 0.02; the bias size is used, the test is not.

- RQ3.4: Freyberger et al. (2025) and Bryzgalova et al. (2025) were read as working-paper versions (BFI 2022, NBER January 2023); the RFS versions were not opened (paywalled).

- RQ3.4: the archived plan is read as: daily bars for full history, 1-hour for 2 years, 5-minute for 1 month, 1-minute for the last 5 days, with an autoencoder filling all missing 1-minute bars back to the start (Autoencoders I, lines 1–30 and 585–900). ## Open questions for the methodology plan

- Which permanent identifier set to key on (CRSP PERMNO/PERMCO if licensed; otherwise a project-built map from CIK, FIGI and LEI) and how to date its spells.
- How to stamp knowledge time for each source: first-seen retrieval time vs published time, and how to keep vintages (storage choice from RP2).
- Which normalisation to use for the autoregressive token model (causal running statistics vs per-window centering vs cross-sectional ranks), to be settled by ablation in RP4's protocol.
- Whether to use fixed-horizon, triple-barrier or trend-scanning labels, chosen on validation data only; which crash definition to use for the shock detector.
- Whether daily-to-intraday reconstruction is dropped entirely (RQ3.4 says it is not supported as training data).
- How to build the company graph as known at each date, and whether LLM-extracted edges are allowed at all for historical periods.
