# RP4 – Training and Evaluation Methodology

## Abstract

RP4 sets out how to train and judge the candidate models so results can be trusted (106 sources: 18 Strong, 61 Moderate, 25 Weak, 2 primary documentation). **Validation (RQ4.1, Medium):** never shuffle; purge and embargo to the label horizon; select inside purged CV or CPCV, and quote results only from an untouched walk-forward segment, corrected for the number of trials. **Tests (RQ4.2, High):** Diebold–Mariano for two non-nested forecasts, Clark–West against nested baselines such as the random walk, SPA for many models against one, the model confidence set for shortlists, FDR/FWER hurdles for searches; heavy tails and few exceedances can distort all of them. **Metrics (RQ4.3, High/Medium):** R²oos, proper scores for distributions, QLIKE on realised variance, two-stage VaR/ES backtests, and lead time at a preset false-alarm budget for shocks. **Training (RQ4.4, Medium):** expect ties with a strong simple model; use a strong price base, small gated add-ons, many seeds and a counted search. **Shock studies (RQ4.5, Medium):** a mechanical event rule, first-public dating, placebos, recursive evaluation, pretrained cutoffs before the test window. **Economic value (RQ4.6, High)** depends on turnover, tradable universe and investor size, not gross accuracy. **Reproducibility (RQ4.7, Medium):** pinned code and data, logged seeds and optimisers, a full trial log, a second backtest engine. **Pretraining (RQ4.8) and adaptation (RQ4.9), Low:** no objective is shown to beat next-token or direct training under a strong financial test; beyond periodic retraining and adaptive window choice, no adaptation method reliably helps market forecasts, and non-finance audits trace most reported gains to label leakage.

## Research questions

- **RQ4.1** – How do walk-forward, purged k-fold and combinatorial purged cross-validation compare at preventing false discoveries?
- **RQ4.2** – Which statistical tests compare forecasts and correct for testing many models? Candidates: Diebold–Mariano, model confidence set, Reality Check / SPA, multiple-testing hurdles
- **RQ4.3** – Which metrics fit each output: returns, volatility, tail risk and shock detection (lead time, precision, false alarms)?
- **RQ4.4** – How should multimodal models be trained when the signal is weak? Consider staged training with frozen encoders, regularisation, ensembling and limits on hyperparameter search
- **RQ4.5** – How should shock detectors be evaluated on past events without hindsight? Cover event selection, event-study method, LLM training-data contamination and date-ordered model checkpoints
- **RQ4.6** – When do statistical forecast gains translate into economic value after costs, turnover and capacity limits?
- **RQ4.7** – What reproducibility practices (experiment logging, seeds, determinism, data snapshots) does the literature recommend?
- **RQ4.8** – Which pretraining objectives beyond next-token prediction (masked reconstruction, multi-step trajectories, contrastive, relation prediction) improve financial foundation models? How are several losses balanced?
- **RQ4.9** – How should a model adapt to new companies, regimes and events without full retraining? Compare context or memory updates, parameter-efficient adapters (LoRA) and MAML-inspired meta-learning (first-order MAML, Reptile, meta-learned adapters); cover drift detection, forgetting, rollback and leak-free evaluation of adaptation

RP4 must include: a recommended evaluation protocol per output type, a metrics table, a short guide to the statistical tests and when each applies, and a protocol for a shock-detection study.

## Findings

### RQ4.1 – How do walk-forward, purged k-fold and combinatorial purged cross-validation compare at preventing false discoveries?

**Answer.** No split scheme prevents false discoveries on its own. Two things decide it: whether training data sits close to test data in time, and whether the search over many models is nested and corrected. (1) **Shuffled k-fold is unusable** on serially dependent data or overlapping labels. On pure noise it reported an information coefficient of +0.32 where the truth is 0 ([[Li 2026 Impossible Trinity Time-Series Validation]]). It "found" R² of 0.83–0.91 on a target built to be unpredictable ([[purgedcv 2026 Purged Cross-Validation Library]]). Building input windows before splitting made LSTM error look up to 20.5% lower under unpurged 10-fold CV, against at most 5% for chronological splits ([[Albelali 2025 Hidden Leaks LSTM Validation]]). (2) **Purging and an embargo remove most mechanical leakage, but only on stationary data.** On the same noise, contiguous 5-fold gave +0.004 and purged 5-fold +0.000. Spurious skill fell with the distance between a test point and the nearest later training point, reaching zero before the label horizon: the closeness of future data matters, not its amount ([[Li 2026 Impossible Trinity Time-Series Validation]]). For correctly specified autoregressions with uncorrelated errors, k-fold CV estimated error more precisely than a single hold-out ([[Bergmeir 2018 Validity of Cross-Validation Autoregressive]]). But CV stays biased in finite samples even then ([[Lusompa 2025 Finite Sample Bias Time Series CV]]), and purging cannot repair non-stationarity ([[Li 2026 Impossible Trinity Time-Series Validation]]). On 62 real series, CV variants were optimistic, and time-ordered hold-outs estimated future error best on the non-stationary half ([[Cerqueira 2020 Evaluating Time Series Forecasting Models]]). (3) **CPCV turns one backtest into many paths.** That is what makes PBO and deflated Sharpe statistics computable. The one direct comparison found by this search reports the lowest PBO and best DSR for CPCV, walk-forward worst, and purged k-fold about equal to k-fold. It was read from the abstract only ([[Arian 2024 Backtest Overfitting ML Era]]). In a DRL case, PBO was 17.5% with walk-forward selection against 8.0% with combinatorial CV. But a low PBO did not stop an agent that lost more than equal weight from passing ([[Gort 2022 DRL Crypto Backtest Overfitting]]). Across resampled walk-forward paths, the spread of out-of-sample Sharpe ratios was as large as the mean (0.36 ± 0.38) ([[skfolio 2026 Cross-Validation Model Selection]]). (4) **Walk-forward is the only strictly causal scheme**, so it is the right final test when live performance is to be quoted. Its weakness is the variance of a single path, not bias ([[Li 2026 Impossible Trinity Time-Series Validation]], [[Cerqueira 2020 Evaluating Time Series Forecasting Models]]). (5) **Selection is the larger false-discovery channel.** The best of K in-sample results grows like √(2 ln K) under the null, so about 100 trials already look significant. The protocol must therefore be nested, with selection inside and an outer, strictly causal segment that no selection touches ([[Li 2026 Impossible Trinity Time-Series Validation]], [[Nikolopoulos 2026 Spurious Predictability Financial ML]]). The best result must be deflated for the number of trials, by PBO ([[Bailey 2017 Probability of Backtest Overfitting]]) or the deflated Sharpe ratio ([[Bailey 2014 Deflated Sharpe Ratio]]). All trials must be reported, and hold-out alone ignores how many were run ([[Bailey 2017 Probability of Backtest Overfitting]]). Running the whole pipeline on synthetic nulls (noise, GARCH, regime volatility, bid–ask bounce) catches leaks and timing errors that a split cannot ([[Nikolopoulos 2026 Spurious Predictability Financial ML]]). The size of validation inflation varies by case: in one volatility study, random CV and walk-forward gave almost the same R² (ratio 1.006) ([[Nikolopoulos 2026 Spurious Predictability Financial ML]]). RL papers mostly still report one backtest path ([[Pippas 2025 RL in Quantitative Finance Survey]]).

**Confidence:** Medium that shuffled k-fold must not be used, that purges and embargoes should be sized to the label horizon plus the dependence half-life, and that model choice must be nested inside an untouched, strictly causal final test with a correction for the number of trials. Several independent sources agree and the mechanisms are clear. Low for ranking CPCV against walk-forward at preventing false discoveries: the only direct comparison was read as an abstract, and the DRL case is one two-month crypto window.

### RQ4.2 – Which statistical tests compare forecasts and correct for testing many models? Candidates: Diebold–Mariano, model confidence set, Reality Check / SPA, multiple-testing hurdles

**Answer.** No single test answers both "is this forecast better than the baseline?" and "is it still better after everything I tried?". The evidence supports a layered set, chosen by the comparison being made. (1) **Two forecasts, non-nested: Diebold–Mariano (DM)** on a loss chosen for the target, with a HAC standard error. Its only assumption is a covariance-stationary loss differential, and it compares forecasts, not models ([[Diebold 2015 Comparing Predictive Accuracy Twenty Years Later]]). (2) **Any model against a random walk or zero forecast is a nested comparison.** Plain DM is then badly undersized: median size 0.008 at nominal 10%, with power 0.031 on a return-like process. The Clark–West adjusted statistic gave 0.080 and 0.190 ([[Clark 2007 Tests for Nested Model Predictive Accuracy]]). Applied finance papers still run plain DM against naive baselines ([[Noguer i Alonso 2026 Pretrained TSFMs Return Forecasting]]). (3) **Many models against one benchmark: Hansen's SPA test, not White's Reality Check.** The Reality Check loses power as poor models are added: about 5.5% power against 99.7% for SPA in one design, and on 3,656 inflation models RC p = .963 against SPA p = .100 ([[Hansen 2005 Test for Superior Predictive Ability]]). The finance template is the 7,846-rule study: a nominal p of 0.004 for the best rule after 1986 became 0.341 once the whole rule universe was counted ([[Sullivan 1999 Data-Snooping Technical Trading Bootstrap]]). (4) **Many models, no privileged benchmark: the model confidence set (MCS)**, which keeps every model the data cannot separate from the best and grows when data are uninformative ([[Hansen 2011 Model Confidence Set]]). (5) **A search over many signals or configurations needs a multiple-testing hurdle.** Control FDR or FWER (BHY, Holm, Bonferroni) and count unreported trials. In the factor literature this puts the bar near t ≈ 3.0, and higher if most trials are hidden ([[Harvey 2016 Cross-Section Expected Returns Multiple Testing]]). For trading results, use the deflated Sharpe ratio or PBO (RQ4.1; [[Bailey 2014 Deflated Sharpe Ratio]], [[Bailey 2017 Probability of Backtest Overfitting]]). Under the null the best of K grows like √(2 ln K) ([[Nikolopoulos 2026 Spurious Predictability Financial ML]]). Pairwise DM with a Bonferroni correction is a simple, conservative option when the set is small ([[Gu 2020 Empirical Asset Pricing Machine Learning]]).

**Where the standard tests break in this project's data.** (a) *Heavy tails.* When loss differentials have infinite variance, a nominal 5% DM test can reject a true null up to 70% of the time at any sample size. In an FX risk study, accounting for this overturned a "GARCH dominates" verdict in favour of simple rolling-window forecasts. Subsampling inference is the fix ([[Frederiksen 2026 Heavy Tails Predictive Ability Testing]]). (b) *Tail and shock forecasts.* Only exceedance days carry information, so the effective sample is the number of exceedances: about six in a 250-day window at 97.5%. With few exceedances, DM favours the less conservative forecaster whichever is better. In seven stock indices, 18.4% of significant DM wins for the less conservative model contradicted the ranking on the remaining days, against 6.9% for the more conservative one. Block bootstrap-t DM or an anytime-valid confidence sequence holds error control ([[Coelho 2026 Tail Forecast Comparison Exceedances]]). DM and MCS have little power against models that understate 1%–2.5% tail risk until several years of daily data are available. Up to about 1,000 days the MCS can eliminate the true model more often than worse ones ([[Bauer 2025 DM and MCS Tail Risk Finite Sample]]). Joint VaR–ES scoring is more powerful than VaR alone from two years on ([[Bauer 2025 DM and MCS Tail Risk Finite Sample]]); large combined pools of tail models are evaluated with coverage backtests plus joint scores ([[Taylor 2025 Combining VaR and ES Forecasts]]).

**Confidence:** High for the core mapping (DM for two forecasts; Clark–West against nested baselines; SPA rather than Reality Check for many against one; MCS for sets; FDR/FWER and trial counting for searches). These are standard results with consistent simulation evidence. Medium for the tail and heavy-tail cautions: the direction is consistent across three 2025–2026 papers, but two are single-author preprints and the exact distortion sizes depend on the DGP.

**What would change the answer:** a finance simulation that compares SPA, MCS and Romano–Wolf stepwise tests with deep-learning model sets and heavy-tailed returns; evidence that bootstrap-t DM keeps size for multi-step, cross-sectional (panel) forecast comparisons; a published rule for trial counting in hyperparameter searches.

**Stop rule:** 15 sources (9 new, 6 reused: 3 Strong, 9 Moderate, 3 Weak). Cap met. The last three new sources (Hansen 2005, Sullivan 1999, Clark–West) each added a finding, so the stop rule was met by the cap, not by saturation. ### RQ4.3 – Which metrics fit each output: returns, volatility, tail risk and shock detection (lead time, precision, false alarms)?

**Answer.** Each output needs a loss that is consistent for the quantity forecast, and a report that shows more than the average. (1) **Returns (point or sign).** Use MSE-based out-of-sample R² against a zero or historical-mean forecast, tested with Clark–West because the benchmark is nested (RQ4.2). Monthly gains are around 0.4% R²oos in the best panel study ([[Gu 2020 Empirical Asset Pricing Machine Learning]]). Accuracy on a labelled proxy is not evidence of return skill: twelve sentiment tools differed widely on labelled accuracy, yet none was related to next-day returns ([[Bisharat 2026 Evaluating Financial Sentiment AI]]). A competition metric that weighted test cases by news agreement made results hard to read ([[Lee 2026 XForecast News-Grounded Challenge]]). For a probabilistic return forecast, score the whole distribution with a strictly proper rule (log score, CRPS, quantile or interval scores). Improper scores reward wrongly dispersed forecasts ([[Gneiting 2007 Strictly Proper Scoring Rules]]). (2) **Volatility.** Only losses in Patton's robust family, chiefly MSE and QLIKE, rank forecasts correctly when the target is a noisy proxy. With daily squared returns, MAE prefers a forecast of about 0.45σ² and MSE on logs about 0.28σ². A 5-minute realised-variance proxy removes almost all the distortion ([[Patton 2011 Volatility Forecast Comparison Imperfect Proxies]]). QLIKE plus MCS is the working standard in the strongest RP1 volatility study ([[Brini 2026 TSFMs vs Econometric Realized Volatility]]). Two cautions apply. The training loss shifts forecast level: a raw loss effect 2.9 times the model effect fell to 0.67 after a validation-only level alignment ([[Tokajuk 2026 Loss Choice Crypto Volatility]]). And aggregate RMSE hides regime bias: models with near-equal RMSE ranked differently on stress-regime bias and tail-weighted loss ([[Chagas 2026 Latent-Regime Bias Auditing Volatility]]). So report bias by regime and an underprediction-weighted loss beside QLIKE. (3) **Tail risk (VaR, ES, intervals).** Use two stages. First, a calibration backtest: Kupiec and Christoffersen coverage and independence tests, or conditional calibration tests ([[Aich 2025 Temporal Conformal Prediction Risk]], [[Nolde 2017 Elicitability and Backtesting]]). Second, a comparative backtest with a consistent score: tick loss for VaR and a 0-homogeneous Fissler–Ziegel score for the (VaR, ES) pair, since ES alone is not elicitable ([[Nolde 2017 Elicitability and Backtesting]]). Many misspecified models pass calibration tests ([[Nolde 2017 Elicitability and Backtesting]]). Report the number of exceedances, which is the real sample size ([[Coelho 2026 Tail Forecast Comparison Exceedances]]); at 1%–2.5% levels, DM and MCS statistics are skewed and can pick the wrong model on up to two years of daily data, so several years are needed; joint VaR–ES scoring adds power over VaR alone from two years on ([[Bauer 2025 DM and MCS Tail Risk Finite Sample]]). For a live monitor, e-backtests are valid at any stopping time, at some cost in power ([[Wang 2025 E-backtesting]]). (4) **Shock detection.** Report lead time (first alarm to event start), hit rate, false-alarm rate per period, and a threshold-free AUROC. Drop the first two post-event years so detection after onset does not count as warning, and require a signal at least about one year and at most five years ahead for slow fragility gauges ([[Drehmann 2014 Evaluating Early Warning Indicators]]). Prefer real-time recursive evaluation, where a plain logit beat random forests out of sample ([[Beutel 2019 ML vs Logit Banking Crisis Warning]]). For rare events, F1, MCC and accuracy push optimal thresholds towards "never alarm", and AUC saturates. Fix the false-alarm tolerance in advance ([[Nikolopoulos 2025 Rare-Event-Stable Evaluation Metrics]]). Lead times from a handful of events tuned with hindsight are not evidence ([[Neela 2025 AIMM Social Media Manipulation Detection]]).

**Confidence:** High for robust volatility losses (MSE, QLIKE) and for two-stage tail backtesting with a joint VaR–ES score: the theory is settled and the empirical work agrees. Medium for shock-detection metrics: AUROC with timing windows is the policy standard, but crisis samples are small (25 crises) and rare-event metric proposals are recent. Medium for reporting regime-conditional bias: two 2026 crypto/ETF studies.

**Disagreement.** Drehmann and Juselius favour AUROC because it assumes no policy loss. Nikolopoulos shows AUC saturates near 1 as prevalence falls. Both points hold. AUROC suits slow indicators judged over multi-year windows with tens of events. A daily shock detector with very rare positives needs a fixed false-alarm budget and lead-time reporting instead.

**What would change the answer:** a finance study comparing shock-detector metrics (AUROC, rare-event-stable, lead-time-weighted) on the same detectors and events; evidence on which proper score best ranks sampled token-path forecasts in fat-tailed returns; a QLIKE-vs-tail-loss comparison on US equities with a long sample.

**Stop rule:** 17 sources (7 new, 10 reused: 2 Strong, 9 Moderate, 6 Weak). Cap of 15 exceeded because Lee 2026 and Bisharat 2026 were tagged RQ4.3 in RP1 and are kept for the returns row. The last three new sources (Patton, Drehmann, Gneiting) each added a metric, so saturation was not reached. ### RQ4.4 – How should multimodal models be trained when the signal is weak? Consider staged training with frozen encoders, regularisation, ensembling and limits on hyperparameter search

**Answer.** Under a weak signal, training should start from the assumption that most configurations will tie with a strong simple model. The training design then decides which of many equally good functions is returned. (1) **Expect a tie, and measure across seeds.** On 564,795 held-out S&P 500 volatility forecasts, 12 architecture–optimizer pairs were statistically tied in 59 of 66 comparisons after Holm correction, and none beat HAR (NMSE 0.5671). Yet the optimizer moved predictions 1.3–7.8 times further than reruns, and volatility portfolios built from tied models differed by up to 58% in turnover ([[Cortesi 2026 Optimizer as Implicit Prior]]). Across 100 seeds per configuration, time-series forecasters had non-Gaussian error distributions and about three times as many underperforming outliers as image models (2.83 vs 1.0 per replicate), so 3–10 seeds do not describe the spread. Early stopping gave the best balance of mean and robustness ([[Coakley 2026 Deep Learning Performance Distributions]]). (2) **Regularise towards the simple benchmark, and tune the penalty causally.** In the "virtue of complexity" market-timing setting, a penalty chosen by rolling CV inside each window went to its maximum in 42–48% of months. The model then collapsed to the historical average, ridgeless fits had R²oos of −0.01 to −0.02, and the apparent alpha disappeared once the historical-average portfolio was controlled for. The original result used a penalty fixed from the full sample ([[Guo 2026 Benign Overfitting Equity Returns]]). For added inputs, randomly zeroing whole covariate channels in training (p = 0.3) kept models near the price-only floor when covariates were corrupted, at little clean-data cost ([[Hu 2026 Exogenous Dropout Corruption-Robust Covariates]]). Naive addition or concatenation of text embeddings often did worse than no text, while gated or low-rank residual injection did better ([[Lee 2026 Constrained Fusion Text Time Series]]). (3) **Stage the model: strong base first, then small, bounded add-ons.** Regime information helped only as small residual experts routed on top of a frozen base network. Appending it to the inputs made MLPs collapse in 30 of 30 seeds and a standard MoE in 24 of 30, and the final gain over a capacity-matched MLP was small (IC 0.547 vs 0.542; HAR 0.532) ([[Ye 2026 Regime-Gated Residual MoE Volatility]]). Separate modality experts with cross-modal attention only in later layers beat shared parameters and early fusion ([[Koval 2025 Modality-Specific Experts Text and TS]]). A frozen TimesFM was no better than the historical mean, and most of the gain came from a random-forest residual stage ([[Dewage 2026 Hybrid Correction Frozen TSFM]]). Plain fine-tuning of Kronos on target data could make it worse than the frozen model ([[Nguyen 2026 Volatility-Clustering Adaptation]]). Pre-training on an index rescued a stock transformer that would not train from scratch (BCE 0.6942 → 0.6404), but XGBoost still had the best F1 and much higher portfolio returns ([[Coolsaet 2026 Index Pre-Training Stock Transformers]]). (4) **Make text earn its place with ablations.** On financial news, no fusion mechanism showed context-attributable gains and a repeat-last-value baseline beat a large MoE model ([[Zhou 2026 When Context Routing Helps Fusion]]). Replacing the text fed to three multimodal forecasters with empty, shuffled or oracle text moved MSE by under 0.5% ([[Sridhar 2026 Text Sensitivity Audit Multimodal TS]]). A 100K-parameter attention fusion model did worse than chance, while a simple anomaly score added to market features did best (AUC 0.680 vs 0.608) ([[Qian 2026 Extreme Volatility Warning Anomaly Fusion]]). (5) **Ensemble across seeds and across weakly correlated model types.** Averaging portfolio weights over more seeds (1 → 64) raised the median Sharpe and narrowed its spread, though in rolling universes the ensemble only roughly matched equal- and value-weighted portfolios ([[Kim 2025 Deep Ensembles Portfolio DSL]]). A rank-average of XGBoost and TabNet, chosen for low error correlation, did better than either alone in one held-out year. No tabular deep model beat XGBoost on its own ([[Le Grice 2026 Cross-Regime Bayesian Optimisation]]). (6) **Limit and count the search.** The one study found that designs its tuning against overfitting fixed the objective's weights before experiments, used 30 trials per model across three regime folds with penalties for any losing fold, and ran a noise-input null check (Sharpe −1.44 vs 2.44). It still did not deflate its result for the model and ensemble search ([[Le Grice 2026 Cross-Regime Bayesian Optimisation]]). The trial-count corrections in RQ4.1–RQ4.2 (√(2 ln K) inflation, DSR, PBO) apply to every hyperparameter search.

**Confidence:** Medium that a weak-signal multimodal model should be built as a strong price base plus small gated or residual add-ons, with channel dropout, causal penalty tuning and text ablations. Several independent 2026 studies agree, one of them Strong, and the failure modes (collapse, no text use) recur. Medium that results must be reported over many seeds and judged by decision-level behaviour, not small loss gaps (one Strong finance study plus one large non-finance study). Low on how large ensembling gains are and on budget rules for hyperparameter search: one Moderate portfolio study, one single-year MSc study, and no finance study that varies the search budget against an overfitting measure.

### RQ4.5 – How should shock detectors be evaluated on past events without hindsight? Cover event selection, event-study method, LLM training-data contamination and date-ordered model checkpoints

**Answer.** Hindsight enters a shock study through four doors: which events are chosen, how they are dated, how significance is computed when events hit many assets at once, and what the model's pretrained parts already know. Each needs its own control. (1) **Fix the event set by a mechanical rule before seeing detector output, and report how sensitive results are to that rule.** In one crypto study, a variance response 3.49 times larger for infrastructure than regulatory shocks under curated, high-salience event choice fell to about 0.5–1.6 times under a mechanical impact screen, and 13 of the 50 curated events failed the study's own strict screen ([[Farzulla 2026 Crypto Event Study Dependence-Robust]]). Report the base rate too: 83% of resolved prediction markets resolved NO, so a constant guess scored well until outcomes were balanced ([[Ye 2026 Hindcast Replaying Prediction Markets]]). (2) **Date events by when the information first became public, and test against placebo dates.** Events should be stamped with the UTC time of first public filing or announcement, checked against primary sources (one dating error was caught that way) ([[Farzulla 2026 Crypto Event Study Dependence-Robust]]). Across seven BTC crashes, an in-sample precursor pattern reversed out of sample, and only one signal survived a 300-onset placebo test, as a population-level rather than per-event signal. Sudden-news shocks showed no price precursor at all ([[Garcia Seuma 2026 Crypto Cascade Early Warning]]). (3) **Evaluate recursively in pseudo real time, with timing windows fixed in advance.** Drop the post-event period so detecting a shock after onset does not count as warning, and set the warning window before testing ([[Drehmann 2014 Evaluating Early Warning Indicators]]). In recursive quasi-real-time tests, a plain logit beat machine-learning models on banking crises ([[Beutel 2019 ML vs Logit Banking Crisis Warning]]). Crisis signals can predict probability but not timing: the best credit-and-price signal missed 36–60% of crises, and more than half its alarms were false ([[Greenwood 2022 Predictable Financial Crises]]). For a live monitor, e-backtests stay valid at any stopping time ([[Wang 2025 E-backtesting]]). (4) **Test "unprecedented" events by holding out the whole event class.** A weather model trained without Category 3–5 cyclones could not forecast Category 5 storms. It predicted moderate storms instead, gave no sign of error, and standard skill scores hid the failure ([[Sun 2024 AI Weather Gray Swan Cyclones]]). (5) **Measure market impact with short windows and dependence-aware inference.** Factor-model abnormal returns stay reliable for short windows when event timing is random. They become biased in volatile periods, over long horizons and when timing tracks market conditions, which describes most shocks. Synthetic-control or matched counterfactuals remove much of that bias: a 0.60 beta gap in a 2008 event window implied about 4.1% bias on one day ([[Goldsmith-Pinkham 2025 Causal Inference Event Studies]]). When many assets share an event date, even low cross-correlation makes standard tests over-reject ([[Kolari 2010 Event Study Cross-Sectional Correlation]]). A naive test treating six correlated assets as independent rejected 35.1% of true nulls at 5%, against 4.6% for a dependence-aware bootstrap; the effective sample was 1.33–2.35, not 6 ([[Farzulla 2026 Crypto Event Study Dependence-Robust]]). (6) **Every pretrained component must have a training cutoff before the test period.** Frontier LLMs recall pre-cutoff index levels and prices almost exactly, and neither instructions nor masking stops this ([[Lopez-Lira 2025 LLM Memorization Problem]]). Date-ordered checkpoints make the test possible. With annual-vintage models, a vintage that had seen the outcome period added a significant "lookahead premium" ([[Yan 2026 DatedGPT Time-Aware Pretraining]]). For time-series foundation models, vintages that had seen post-origin data changed forecasts, usually for the worse (higher MSFE in 18 of 20 US cases). The exposure therefore breaks the information set, but it does not always inflate results ([[Chen 2026 Look-Ahead Bias Pretrained Forecasting Models]]). Pre-registered, blinded, identity-masked testing on a point-in-time universe cut one validated signal from IC 0.065 to 0.020 ([[Dehkordi 2026 OLIVIA LLM News Look-Ahead Test]]). The cleanest design logs predictions live, before outcomes exist ([[Chen 2026 Agentic AI Nowcasting Returns]]). A replay against an immutable archive, with the cutoff enforced as a strict filter in the data layer and a matched-time market price as benchmark, closes the retrieval channel but not the weights channel ([[Ye 2026 Hindcast Replaying Prediction Markets]]).

**Confidence:** Medium that a hindsight-free shock study needs a pre-set mechanical event rule, first-public timestamps, placebo dates, recursive evaluation with post-event exclusion, and dependence-aware inference. Each step has independent support, including two Strong studies, though much of it comes from crypto, banking crises or non-finance settings. Medium that every pretrained component needs a cutoff before the test period, with date-ordered checkpoints: four finance studies agree, but they measure the effect in different units. Low on how much hindsight inflates reported lead times for market shock detectors: no study measures it directly.

### RQ4.6 – When do statistical forecast gains translate into economic value after costs, turnover and capacity limits?

**Answer.** A statistical gain becomes economic value only when it survives three filters, in this order: the cost of trading on it, the tradable universe and investor size, and the comparison with a simple portfolio. Most published gains fail at least one. (1) **Turnover decides whether a gross edge survives costs.** On a CRSP daily panel, ridge had the best gross long–short Sharpe (3.88) and plain OLS 2.48. At 20 bps one-way, OLS fell to −2.32 while slow-rotating LSTM and transformer models kept about 0.5–0.6 ([[Zhang 2026 Deep TS Models Equity Portfolios]]). In hourly Bitcoin trading, every naive sign strategy that was profitable gross lost 50–99% a year at 10 bps ([[Bysik 2026 ML Bitcoin Trading Transaction Costs]]). In a 40-year US large-cap backtest, a characteristic sort with gross Sharpe 1.10 had net Sharpe −11.87, and the ML portfolio with the best gross Sharpe (2.00) could not be implemented at all ([[Jensen 2026 ML Implementable Efficient Frontier]]). Models tied on forecast error can differ by up to 58% in turnover ([[Cortesi 2026 Optimizer as Implicit Prior]]). (2) **Gains concentrate where trading is hardest.** Deep-learning profits came from hard-to-arbitrage stocks and volatile periods. Excluding microcaps and distressed stocks, or adding reasonable costs, cut them considerably (abstract only; [[Avramov 2023 ML vs Economic Restrictions]]). In the CRSP benchmark, break-even costs were 24–61 bps for broad decile sorts but at or below a few bps once capacity, beta, industry and turnover constraints were imposed. Every constrained model then had negative net Sharpe at 20 bps, and restricting to the 500 largest stocks turned the best combinations negative ([[Zhang 2026 Deep TS Models Equity Portfolios]]). Net Sharpe falls with investor size, because costs grow with position size ([[Jensen 2026 ML Implementable Efficient Frontier]]). Gu et al.'s monthly gains (equal-weighted NN Sharpe 2.45, 1.69 without microcaps, 1.35 value-weighted) are gross of costs ([[Gu 2020 Empirical Asset Pricing Machine Learning]]). (3) **Cost-aware design recovers part of the value; cost-blind forecasting does not.** Trading only when the forecast exceeds twice the cost cut turnover by more than tenfold and restored profit in some configurations ([[Bysik 2026 ML Bitcoin Trading Transaction Costs]]). Weekly instead of daily rebalancing raised ridge net Sharpe from 0.60 to 1.02 ([[Zhang 2026 Deep TS Models Equity Portfolios]]). Learning portfolio weights with costs in the objective gave net Sharpe 1.38 against 1.11–1.16 for tuned two-step benchmarks, and it relied on slow signals (value, quality) rather than short-term reversal ([[Jensen 2026 ML Implementable Efficient Frontier]]). Theory gives the reason: with costs, a signal's value depends on how well it forecasts the slow, cheap-to-trade part of future returns, so signals equal in accuracy can differ widely in value ([[Smerlak 2026 Optimal Trading Filters Signal Value]]). (4) **After costs, the bar is the simple portfolio, not the gross baseline.** The best cost-aware Bitcoin strategy did not significantly beat buy-and-hold ([[Bysik 2026 ML Bitcoin Trading Transaction Costs]]). A transformer policy trained on a Sharpe loss only matched equal weighting on 16 futures (net Sharpe 0.54 vs 0.52) ([[Pollok 2026 End-to-End Policies vs Simple Rules]]). On multi-asset futures the leading models kept their ranking at 0–5 bp, while iTransformer and Mamba2 lost their gross advantage ([[Saly-Kaufmann 2026 Deep Learning Financial Benchmark]]). (5) **The final test is prospective.** Among 32 crypto methods, the backtest ranking nearly reversed in paper trading (median return 36.0% to −9.8%). None of four survivors beat Bitcoin in real-money trading ([[Yu 2026 AI Crypto Backtest to Live Gap]]). Selection over many rules must be corrected before any net figure is quoted ([[Sullivan 1999 Data-Snooping Technical Trading Bootstrap]]; RQ4.2). A single held-out year after model and ensemble selection is not evidence of value ([[Le Grice 2026 Cross-Regime Bayesian Optimisation]]). A review of the field found no AI architecture with persistent, capacity-aware net alpha across regimes ([[Zhu 2026 AI Equity Crypto Limits Review]]). (6) **The backtest engine is itself a cost-side error source.** On identical data, five engines agreed exactly at zero cost, but diverged in proportion to turnover once costs were on (up to 3.71 percentage points of total return for rotation strategies). One engine's default silently cut commissions 100-fold ([[Yin 2026 Implementation Risk Backtest Engines]]).

**Confidence:** High that turnover and the tradable universe, not forecast accuracy, decide whether a gain is economically real, and that gross results must not be quoted as value. Two Strong and two Moderate studies across equities, futures and crypto agree, and the mechanism is clear. Medium that cost-aware training or execution (economic objective, trade thresholds, slower rebalancing) recovers a useful share of the value: one Moderate large-cap study ([[Jensen 2026 ML Implementable Efficient Frontier]], read through a summary) and one single-asset crypto study. Low on how much any specific model class keeps after costs at the project's likely scale: no study tests autoregressive token or multimodal models net of costs on a liquid universe.

### RQ4.7 – What reproducibility practices (experiment logging, seeds, determinism, data snapshots) does the literature recommend?

**Answer.** The literature asks for more than "fix the seed". Reproducibility fails at four levels, and each needs its own practice. (1) **Code and data: rerunning the same code on the same data often fails.** Across 1,000+ finance results from 168 teams, rerunning the authors' own code on the same raw data gave exactly the same results only 52% of the time. Reproducibility was lower for complex code and tail results, and researchers overestimated their own reproducibility (abstract only; [[Perignon 2024 Computational Reproducibility Finance]]). The practice that follows is a pinned environment, fixed scripts and deterministic splits, as in the CRSP benchmark that listed its software versions and fixed its bootstrap seed ([[Zhang 2026 Deep TS Models Equity Portfolios]]). (2) **Hardware and framework: seeds are not enough.** Floating-point reduction order on GPUs, sampling-based explainers and parallel inference settings change outputs even with seeds fixed. On a Bitcoin fraud graph, 15.5–27.5% of nodes changed their modal label across 50 seeds. Deterministic GPU flags removed one source at about five times the memory ([[Zhou 2026 Determinism Financial AI Survey]], Weak; its earlier versions contained errors). So record the hardware and determinism settings, and treat bit-exact replay as a separate goal from stable conclusions. (3) **Training randomness: report distributions, not single runs.** Over 100 seeds, time-series forecasters had fat-tailed error distributions with about three times as many bad outliers as image models, so 3–10 seeds understate the spread ([[Coakley 2026 Deep Learning Performance Distributions]]). With initialisation and data order held fixed, the optimiser alone moved predictions 1.3–7.8 times more than reruns did ([[Cortesi 2026 Optimizer as Implicit Prior]]). The optimiser, the seeds and their number are part of what must be logged. (4) **Analysis choices and simulation code: the largest hidden spread.** When 164 teams tested the same hypotheses on the same data, the spread of their estimates was about double the standard error. Better reproducibility and peer feedback reduced it, and most teams underestimated it ([[Menkveld 2024 Nonstandard Errors]]). Five backtest engines given identical inputs agreed exactly at zero cost but diverged with turnover once costs were on. One engine had a default that cut commissions 100-fold, and comparing engines was what exposed such defects ([[Yin 2026 Implementation Risk Backtest Engines]]). Every trial must be logged so overfitting can be measured ([[Bailey 2017 Probability of Backtest Overfitting]]), and the whole pipeline should be run on synthetic nulls to catch leaks and timing errors ([[Nikolopoulos 2026 Spurious Predictability Financial ML]]). **Data snapshots** have no direct study in this set. RP2 and RP3 already require immutable raw data with first-seen timestamps and vintages, which is the snapshot the experiments must point to ([[RP2 Data Sources and Storage]], [[RP3 Cleaning and Labelling]]).

**Confidence:** Medium that a reproducible protocol needs a pinned environment and data snapshot, logged seeds, optimiser and hardware settings, many seeds with reported distributions, a full trial log, a second backtest engine or a reference cost implementation, and null-pipeline audits. Each element has direct support (two Strong studies, two Moderate), but the finance meta-research was read partly from an abstract and a summary. Low on specific tooling (experiment trackers, data-versioning systems): no study compares them, and the guide's "experiment tracking best practices" query found no finance evidence.

### RQ4.8 – Which pretraining objectives beyond next-token prediction (masked reconstruction, multi-step trajectories, contrastive, relation prediction) improve financial foundation models? How are several losses balanced?

**Answer.** No pretraining objective has been shown to beat next-token or direct supervised training on financial targets under a strong protocol. The evidence (14 sources, mostly single-split or non-finance) supports five narrower findings. (1) **Reconstruct smooth, derived targets, not raw values, and mask in contiguous blocks.** In finance, masking raw prices transfers poorly. Predicting masked moving averages beat predicting masked values (NASDAQ Sharpe 1.97 vs 1.54) ([[Wang 2025 SSPT Stock-Customised Pretraining Tasks]]). Reconstructing 77 technical-indicator targets beat masked reconstruction (PatchTST), contrastive (TS2Vec, TimesURL, FASCL) and autoregressive (Kronos-small) pretraining on average across six markets (long-only Sharpe 1.40 vs 1.01, 0.98, 0.91, 0.95 and 1.26). None of the financial pretraining methods beat generic PatchTST ([[Chen 2026 DIVINE Indicator Reconstruction Pretraining]]). In a controlled general-domain recipe, masking contiguous blocks of patches cut MASE from 0.750 to 0.696 against scattered masking, while the mask ratio (20–80%) barely mattered ([[Wen 2026 Generic Transformer TSFM Baseline]]). (2) **Data breadth beats objective tweaks and scale.** Pooling six markets was DIVINE's largest single gain (Sharpe 1.18 → 1.40). Enriching the pretraining mix beat scaling model size ([[Wen 2026 Generic Transformer TSFM Baseline]]). A 991M-parameter financial FM (FinCast) did worse than several models trained from scratch, and Kronos-base did worse than Kronos-small ([[Chen 2026 DIVINE Indicator Reconstruction Pretraining]]). (3) **Contrastive, latent-prediction and relation objectives are task- and backbone-dependent.** A contrastive co-movement objective learned useful relation structure: better sector classification and out-of-sample hedges than correlation (hedged volatility 19.1% vs 23.8%). But the best loss for one task was the worst for the other ([[Dolphin 2024 Contrastive Asset Embeddings]]). Identity and sector classification as pretext tasks helped stock selection ([[Wang 2025 SSPT Stock-Customised Pretraining Tasks]]). JEPA pretraining changed forecasting error by between −15% and +12% depending on the backbone ([[Feng 2026 JEPA Pretraining Time Series Forecasting]], Weak). (4) **Multi-step objectives: train for the horizon directly.** One-shot multi-horizon decoding beat autoregressive patch rollout, by up to 10.7% at the longest horizons ([[Kim 2026 Dynamic Multi-Step Decoding TSFMs]]). The financial TSFM ranked first on FinVerse is trained with a direct multi-horizon quantile loss and no rollout ([[Lee 2026 EXAONE Finance Attention-Free TSFM]]). Training on a mixture of label horizons raised IC and Sharpe over training on the target horizon alone ([[Song 2026 Label Horizon Paradox]]). Kronos remains the main next-token counter-example: it is competitive but not best in DIVINE's controlled ranking ([[Shi 2025 Kronos Financial Foundation Model]]). (5) **Pretraining gains are small next to proper controls.** Against architecture-matched random weights, seven released TSFMs (masked, contrastive, self-distillation, multi-task) gained 1.4–5.2 accuracy points on frozen-feature classification. Only one gain survived a Holm correction, and non-pretrained ROCKET-family methods scored higher ([[Sungu Isiacik 2026 Random Initialization TSFM Baseline]], non-finance). Index pretraining let a stock transformer train at all, but XGBoost still earned more ([[Coolsaet 2026 Index Pre-Training Stock Transformers]], Weak).

**Balancing several losses.** Tasks conflict. When three stock pretext tasks were trained together, each did worse than alone; the result swung with the weight on the masked-average loss (γ = 10 helped, γ = 5 did not). The robust recipe was equal weights on the two compatible tasks ([[Wang 2025 SSPT Stock-Customised Pretraining Tasks]]). Auxiliary volume and volatility losses raised CSI300 IC only from 0.0543 to 0.0575. They carried small, validation-tuned weights (0.03–0.35 vs 0.63–0.7 for return), and adding heads without gated transfer helped less ([[Yang 2026 LiMT Liquidity-Aware Multi-Task Stocks]]). The general multi-task benchmark found fixed weights tuned on validation (scalarization) or a simple inverse-loss softmax best. Learned uncertainty weighting overfit, method differences shrank in larger networks, and per-method learning-rate tuning explained most published ranking disagreements ([[Kirchdorfer 2024 Uncertainty-Based Loss Weighting MTL]], non-finance). The practical reading: use few, fixed weights tuned on validation; report each task's own metric under combination to catch negative transfer; let auxiliaries enter the main task through a gate; and count every weight setting in the trial log (RQ4.1, RQ4.2).

**Leakage note.** Pretraining corpora can overlap the test period or test series. Wen et al. measure the gain from a leaky corpus explicitly, and Kronos's corpus runs to June 2024. Pretraining only on synthetic financial series removes real-data contamination by design ([[Lee 2026 EXAONE Finance Attention-Free TSFM]]). FinVerse itself cannot rule out evaluation-period data in other models' pretraining ([[Lee 2026 FinVerse Financial TSFM Benchmark]]).

**Confidence:** Medium that, for financial series, reconstruction of smoothed or derived targets beats raw masked-value reconstruction, that contiguous masking beats scattered masking, and that pretraining-data breadth matters more than model size. These rest on two finance studies with one split each, plus one Strong general-domain study. Medium that pretraining must be judged against an architecture-matched random-init twin and a strong non-pretrained baseline (GBT), given one Strong non-finance study and the Weak finance case. Low on which objective family is best for financial forecasting: no study compares masked, contrastive, multi-step and next-token objectives under walk-forward validation with costs and a random-walk or GBT baseline. Low on loss balancing in finance (two single-split studies; the rest is vision). **What would change it:** a controlled finance comparison of objective families on one corpus and backbone, with walk-forward splits, a point-in-time universe, a random-init twin and a GBT baseline.

### RQ4.9 – How should a model adapt to new companies, regimes and events without full retraining? Compare context or memory updates, parameter-efficient adapters (LoRA) and MAML-inspired meta-learning (first-order MAML, Reptile, meta-learned adapters); cover drift detection, forgetting, rollback and leak-free evaluation of adaptation

**Answer.** No adaptation method has been shown to improve market forecasts reliably under a leak-free test. The evidence (15 sources: 8 new, 7 reused; most of the method evidence is non-finance) supports six findings, and the first one governs how all the others should be read.

(1) **Most reported adaptation gains come from the evaluation, not the method.** An H-step forecast's label exists only H steps later. Protocols descended from FSNet let the adapter train on labels before they exist; FSNet's and OneNet's own code used next-step labels ([[Zhao 2025 PROCEED Proactive Drift Adaptation]]). Under the correct delay, earlier online forecasters' MSE rose by 107% on average, and OneNet went from best to worst on two datasets (same source). When labels were released only after maturity (d ≥ H) inside the released code of four test-time adapters (TAFAS, COSA, PETSA, DynaTTA), the largest significant gain over the frozen model was 0.5% on four of five streams, at least one method was significantly worse than frozen on each, and longer label delays made every adapter that separated from frozen harmful on the most drift-heavy stream. Leaky next-step updates inflated simple adapters' gains by up to 110%, and the backbone training recipe moved frozen error by up to 25×, more than any adaptation effect ([[Fentazi 2026 Delay-Aware Audit Test-Time Adaptation]], Strong, non-finance). A separate leak-free study found leakage added 11–40 points to the measured benefit and flipped its sign in one case. Even without leakage, the benefit moved by 3–19 points with the static baseline's training budget alone, and an optimizer comparison reversed under a shared default learning rate. Choosing both on a held-out slice of the latest pre-adaptation data came within 3.4 points of the test-selected oracle ([[Fujimoto 2026 Leakage-Free Online Adaptation Edge]]).

(2) **The cheapest adaptations are the ones that survive.** A bank of closed-form recursive-least-squares correctors on a frozen model's output (no tuned hyperparameters) beat three of four published gradient adapters at a fraction of their cost; 3–11 of 12 gradient-adapter learning-rate settings diverged per cell ([[Fentazi 2026 Delay-Aware Audit Test-Time Adaptation]]). A linear adapter that predicts a frozen foundation model's residual from the input window and the model's own forecast, mixed in by recent-error weighting, cut MSE by about 6.3% across five TSFMs. White-box and grey-box adapters ported to the same black-box setting made errors worse, and learning from past errors alone hurt (−0.7%). On the one financial series (exchange rates) the gain was 0% ([[Dai 2026 ORCA Black-Box TSFM Adaptation]]). In markets, refreshing only batch-normalisation statistics on recent unlabeled windows gave small Diebold–Mariano-significant gains on SPY, QQQ and EUR/USD, while gradient updates of the normalisation parameters were significantly worse than no adaptation on SPY and QQQ; direction accuracy stayed near 50% ([[Wu 2026 Test-Time Adaptation Non-stationary Markets]]). Adding direction and compounded-price terms to the test-time adaptation loss lowered reconstructed-price MSE on US equity panels in 2023–2025, but with no random-walk baseline and frozen backbones that were often very poor ([[Lian 2026 DPAL Financial Test-Time Adaptation]]).

(3) **Fine-tuning and LoRA adapt the error, not the skill.** LoRA-adapted TimesFM lowered MAE against zero-shot TimesFM (S&P 15.15 vs 16.31) but not against an always-up forecast (15.56). It showed no directional skill at any horizon, and per-sector adapters were significantly worse than one pooled adapter (DM +5.12) ([[Cheung 2026 Base-Rate-Honest TimesFM LoRA Equities]]). Plain next-token fine-tuning of Kronos on target-market data could make it worse than the frozen model ([[Nguyen 2026 Volatility-Clustering Adaptation]], Weak). Sequential fine-tuning always caused forgetting of earlier tasks, less in larger models. Replaying stored past inputs and predictions removed most of it and let a 100k-parameter model match the foundation models on real data ([[Karaouli 2025 Catastrophic Forgetting TSFMs Continual]], non-finance).

(4) **Against regime change, plain periodic retraining and window choice are the baselines to beat.** For RL portfolio agents on three equity universes, fine-tuning, experience replay, EWC and constrained activations all performed the same as rolling retraining (NAS100 24.5–24.6 vs 24.5), and measured forgetting was near zero. Only a library of regime-specific policies switched by a CUSUM change detector added about 15 points, a result not yet replicated ([[Pan 2026 ReCAP Regime-Adaptive Continual Learning]]). In a 3,933-run study, periodic retraining beat error-threshold and ADWIN drift-triggered retraining by 8.6–16.0 points under abrupt and gradual drift (Holm-corrected). Drift triggers won only under recurring drift, and not significantly. With per-sample incremental updates, no policy beat never retraining. Error-threshold triggers spent 66–77% of their budget on pre-drift noise, and ADWIN missed gradual drift and exhausted its budget on false alarms in 51 of 54 calibrations on imbalanced fraud data ([[Dasari 2026 When to Retrain Drift Policies]], Strong, non-market). For industry-portfolio returns, the best training window depends on model complexity. In recessions a ridge model on the last 64 months beat a random forest on all history. Choosing model and window jointly on adaptively chosen recent validation data raised out-of-sample R² by 14–23% and stayed positive in 1990 when fixed-window selection went negative ([[Capponi 2025 Nonstationarity-Complexity Tradeoff Returns]]). For VaR–ES, an adaptive look-back window (keep the longest window not significantly worse than shorter ones) cut joint loss, most of all in the GFC ([[Li 2026 Adaptive Window Selection Risk]]).

(5) **MAML-inspired meta-learning has only weak finance evidence.** MAML over regime clusters of stocks raised zero-shot rank IC on unseen stocks in one 2020 window (S&P 500 GRU 0.026 vs 0.016), but plain pre-training won some cells ([[Liu 2025 Robust Meta-Learning Zero-Shot Financial]], Weak). Reptile-style first-order meta-learning for stocks listed under a year gave small MCC gains over training on the new stocks alone, with accuracy of 51–57% and a −79% a year plain-Reptile trading result over a test window under three months ([[Wang 2023 Meta-Stock Sub-new Stock Meta-Learning]], Weak). No study compares first-order MAML, Reptile or meta-learned adapters against pooled training plus periodic retraining under walk-forward splits and costs; for new companies, the strongest comparator found is one pooled model (Cheung's pooled adapter beat per-sector ones).

(6) **Drift detection, forgetting and rollback.** Detectors are best treated as triggers for a bounded action, not as proof of drift: ADWIN and error thresholds were seed-sensitive and either fired on noise or missed slow drift ([[Dasari 2026 When to Retrain Drift Policies]]). Under delayed labels, choosing between adapted and frozen forecasts by recent error fails because the feedback that would dismiss a failing expert arrives d steps late. A per-coordinate median over a bank that includes a pass-through (frozen) expert absorbed exploding experts ([[Fentazi 2026 Delay-Aware Audit Test-Time Adaptation]]). An error-weighted router that falls back to the frozen forecast kept about 80% of the gain when removed, so most of the value is in the correction itself ([[Dai 2026 ORCA Black-Box TSFM Adaptation]]). In markets, forgetting was near zero for RL agents ([[Pan 2026 ReCAP Regime-Adaptive Continual Learning]]), but no study measures forgetting or rollback for a market forecaster. The evidence on rollback is thin: the closest mechanisms are keeping the frozen model as an always-available expert and switching back when its recent error is lower.

**Leak-free evaluation of adaptation (protocol assembled from the sources).** (a) Release each label for adaptation only at s + d with d ≥ H, plus the real data latency; report d = 1 only as a leaky reference ([[Fentazi 2026 Delay-Aware Audit Test-Time Adaptation]], [[Zhao 2025 PROCEED Proactive Drift Adaptation]]). (b) Score every forecast before its target can enter any update ([[Fujimoto 2026 Leakage-Free Online Adaptation Edge]]). (c) Compare with a validation-tuned frozen model, a periodic-retrain schedule, a closed-form corrector and the RP1 baselines (random walk, HAR, GBT) ([[Fentazi 2026 Delay-Aware Audit Test-Time Adaptation]], [[Dasari 2026 When to Retrain Drift Policies]]). (d) Choose the baseline's training budget, learning rates and any trigger thresholds on the latest pre-test slice only ([[Fujimoto 2026 Leakage-Free Online Adaptation Edge]]). (e) Report adaptation cost, divergence counts and the backbone recipe. (f) Test across seeds and regimes with the RQ4.2 tests and count every adapter setting in the trial log (RQ4.1). (g) For new companies, hold out tickers as well as time ([[Cheung 2026 Base-Rate-Honest TimesFM LoRA Equities]]).

**Confidence:** Medium that adaptation must be judged under delayed-label, score-before-update evaluation against a tuned frozen model, a periodic-retrain schedule and a closed-form corrector. Three independent studies agree (one Strong, one Moderate KDD paper, one Moderate), but none uses market data. Medium that periodic retraining and training-window choice are the baselines a drift-triggered or continual scheme must beat, given one Strong non-market study and two Moderate finance studies (Pan, Capponi). Low on which adaptation method helps a market forecaster: the finance studies (Wu, Cheung, Nguyen, Lian, Pan, Capponi, Li) are single-split or single-period, and none tests a token or foundation model under delayed labels with costs. Low for MAML-inspired meta-learning (two Weak studies). Evidence thin on rollback and on forgetting in market forecasters. **What would change it:** a walk-forward market study with delayed labels that compares frozen, periodic retraining, closed-form correction, LoRA, replay and first-order meta-learning on one backbone, with held-out tickers, random-walk/HAR/GBT baselines, costs and seed spread.

### Observations across the questions

- **The protocol moves results more than the method does.** Shuffled CV produced an IC of +0.32 on noise (RQ4.1); the backbone training recipe moved frozen error up to 25× more than any test-time adapter, and label leakage inflated adaptation gains by up to 110% (RQ4.9); counting the full rule universe turned a nominal p of 0.004 into 0.341 (RQ4.2); 12 architecture–optimizer pairs tied in 59 of 66 comparisons (RQ4.4). In the studies that measured both, protocol effects were larger than method effects.
- **Selection is the common false-discovery channel.** It appears in validation (best of K grows like √(2 ln K)), testing (SPA, MCS, FDR), hyperparameter search, loss weighting, adapter tuning and event curation (a 3.49× effect fell to about 0.5–1.6× under a mechanical screen). One trial log and one correction rule serve all of them.
- **Look-ahead is wider than data leakage.** RP3 covered leaks in the data. RP4 adds three more channels: labels used before they exist (RQ4.9), pretrained weights that have seen the test period (RQ4.5) and pretraining corpora that overlap test series (RQ4.8). Their measured effects differ in sign, so they must be excluded by design, not corrected afterwards.
- **The simple baselines from RP1 keep winning under good protocols:** HAR for volatility, a plain logit for crisis warning, gradient-boosted trees for selection, equal weight or buy-and-hold for portfolios, and periodic retraining or the frozen model for adaptation.
- **Effective samples are small.** The real sample is the number of exceedances (about six in 250 trading days at 97.5%), crises (tens) or independent events (1.33–2.35 effective assets out of six). Tests have little power there, so counts must be reported and inference must allow for dependence.
- **Much of the method evidence is not financial.** The strongest sources on masking, random-init controls, label delay and retraining policy use energy, weather, traffic or credit data. They are used for mechanisms and evaluation design only.

## Open questions for the methodology plan

1. How long is the untouched final walk-forward segment, and how wide are the purge and embargo for daily multi-horizon labels with volatility clustering? (RQ4.1)
2. What counts as a trial, and which correction is applied to the final result: deflated Sharpe, PBO, SPA, or an FDR hurdle on a stated trial count? (RQ4.1, RQ4.2)
3. Which baselines are mandatory per target: random walk, HAR, GARCH-type tail model, gradient-boosted trees, plain logit, equal weight or buy-and-hold, frozen model, periodic retraining? (protocol table)
4. Which realised-variance proxy and training loss for volatility, and how is forecast level aligned without touching the test period? (RQ4.3)
5. The shock definition: the mechanical event rule, the candidate pool, the warning window and the false-alarm budget, fixed before any detector is run. (RQ4.5)
6. The cost model: spread or impact model, assumed investor size, tradable universe, rebalancing frequency, and which second engine or reference implementation checks it. (RQ4.6, RQ4.7)
7. The seed budget per configuration and the compute that implies; how seed distributions are reported. (RQ4.4, RQ4.7)
8. The pretraining ablation set: next-token vs derived-target reconstruction vs direct multi-horizon objectives, each with a random-init twin and a GBT baseline; and whether loss weights are fixed and validation-tuned. (RQ4.8)
9. Whether any adaptation beyond periodic retraining enters the build, and if so the label-delay protocol, held-out tickers and the fallback-to-frozen rule. (RQ4.9)
10. The cutoff policy for pretrained components (e.g. a small text encoder): a cutoff before the test window, date-ordered checkpoints, or training it in-house. (RQ4.5)
11. Tooling for experiment logs and data snapshots: no evidence favours a tool, so the choice rests on convenience and on fitting the RP2 storage design. (RQ4.7)
